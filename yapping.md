# Solving Transformer Bottleneck using Hybrid Mamba Architecture in TTS

Have you ever heard about political figures speaking like that? Actually, this speech are generated by Text-to-Speech (TTS) model. Nowadays, Artificial Intelligence (AI) has made significant advancements in the field of TTS, allowing for the creation of highly realistic and natural-sounding speech. Thanks to attention mechanism (Vaswani et al., 2017) with leveraging deep learning techniques, TTS models can convert written text into spoken words with remarkable accuracy and expressiveness.

Even though TTS models have achieved impressive results, they still face challenges in terms of computational efficiency and latency. Attention mechanism, while powerful, can be computationally expensive, especially when dealing with long sequences of text. This can lead to bottlenecks in the processing pipeline, resulting in slower response times and increased resource consumption.

## Transformer Bottleneck

</div>

To give better understanding about the bottleneck, let's take a look at the transformer architecture. The Transformer architecture is built from repeated blocks containing multi-head self-attention and feed-forward networks, together with residual connections and layer normalization. This process allows model to be trained in parallel, which is a significant advantage over traditional recurrent neural networks (RNNs).

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathrm{Attention}(Q, K, V) &= \mathrm{Softmax}\left(\frac{QK^T}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right)V \\[6pt]
\text{where,} \quad
Q &= \text{Query}, \\
K &= \text{Key}, \\
V &= \text{Value}, \\
d_k &= \text{dimension of the key vectors.}
\end{aligned}
$$

Easy way to understand it, the attention mechanism enables the model to assign different levels of importance to words in the input sequence when producing an output. In this mechanism, the query ($Q$) represents the information the model seeks, the key ($K$) represents the information used for matching relevance, and the value ($V$) contains the actual content to be aggregated. The relevance between Q and K is measured using their similarity score, computed as $QK^T$. After normalization through the softmax function, these scores are used as weights for the value vectors, producing a context-aware representation.

Self-attention is the modification of the attention mechanism where the word paired with itself and other to extract the contextual information from the input sequence which, allows the model to capture long-range dependencies and relationships between words, which is crucial for generating coherent and natural-sounding speech in TTS applications.

However, the self-attention mechanism is computationally intensive to do infererence, especially when dealing with long sequences of text. Mathematically, Full self-attention has $O(n^2)$ time and memory complexity with respect to sequence length nnn, which becomes costly for long sequences., where n is the length of the input sequence. This means that as the length of the input sequence increases, the computational cost grows quadratically, leading to a bottleneck in processing.

For a lustrum, researchers have been exploring various approaches to mitigate the transformer bottleneck in TTS models. There are several techniques that have been proposed to address this issue,

### 1. State Space Models (SSMs)

  <img src="asset/Mamba.png" alt="Alt text" width="500" />
</div>

A major modern deep-learning milestone for SSM-based sequence modeling was S4 by Gu et al. (2021), which made structured state spaces practical for long-sequence learning. They introduced a novel approach to sequence modeling using structured state spaces. Earlier SSM variants often underperformed attention on discrete modalities such as language, but newer selective SSMs like Mamba substantially narrow or even reverse that gap in some settings. The key idea behind SSMs is to represent the sequence as a state space model, where the state at each time step is updated based on the previous state and the input at that time step. Mathematically, the state update can be represented as,

$$
x'(t) = Ax(t) + Bu(t) \\
y(t) = Cx(t) + Du(t)
$$

The idea is to map 1D sequence into a state space representation, where the state at each time step is updated based on the previous state and the input at that time step. The matrices $A$, $B$, $C$, and $D$ are learnable parameters that define the dynamics of the system. By leveraging the properties of state space models, SSMs theoretically faster than traditional attention mechanisms with $O(N)$.

Here's the catch, SSMs are not better than attention mechanism in terms of language modelling. In fact, SSMs have been shown to perform worse than attention mechanisms in certain tasks, such as language modeling. This is because SSMs may struggle to capture complex dependencies and relationships between words in the input sequence, which can lead to a decrease in performance.


### 2. Diffusion Transformer Models

Another recent research direction in text-to-speech synthesis is to combine diffusion models with Transformer-based backbones. In 2023, examples of this line of work included U-DiT TTS: U-Diffusion Vision Transformer for Text-to-Speech and Diffusion Transformer for Adaptive Text-to-Speech, which explored using Transformer architectures as the denoising network inside diffusion-based TTS systems.

Standard sequence-to-sequence TTS models typically use autoregressive. It means that the model generates one output token at a time, conditioning on previously generated tokens. Diffusion models take different approach. Instead of directly predicting the target acoustic representation in one pass, they define a forward noising process that gradually corrupts clean data with Gaussian noise, and then learn a reverse denoising process that reconstructs the clean sample step by step. This process pass through standard formulation of the forward process in Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Models (DDPMs),

$$
q(x_t \mid x_{t-1}) = \mathcal{N}\!\left(x_t;\sqrt{1-\beta_t}\,x_{t-1},\beta_t I\right)
$$

Equivalently, the noisy sample at timestep $t$ can be written in closed form as: 

$$
x_t = \sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_t}\,x_0 + \sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_t}\,\epsilon,\qquad \epsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0,I)
$$

where $x_0$ is the clean sample, $x_t$ is its noisy version at timestep $t$, and $\bar{\alpha}_t$ is the cumulative product of the noise schedule coefficients. During training, the model learns the reverse process $p_\theta(x_{t-1}\mid x_t)$, which iteratively removes noise and reconstructs the target representation. 

In TTS, the diffusion model is typically applied to an acoustic representation, such as a mel spectrogram or another continuous latent speech representation, rather than directly to raw text tokens. For example, U-DiT TTS is a mel-spectrogram-based acoustic model that uses a pretrained HiFi-GAN vocoder to convert the generated spectrogram into waveform audio. 

The role of the Transformer in these models is usually to act as the denoising backbone. In other words, the Transformer processes the noisy acoustic representation together with conditioning information such as text, and predicts how to remove noise at each diffusion step. In adaptive TTS, this conditioning can be injected through mechanisms such as adaptive layer normalization, enabling parameter-efficient speaker or style adaptation. 

However, it is important to state this carefully: diffusion transformers do not inherently solve the Transformer bottleneck. If the denoising backbone still relies on standard full self-attention, then its attention computation remains quadratic with sequence length. In addition, diffusion-based generation usually requires many denoising iterations, which can make inference slower than one-shot or low-step alternatives unless additional acceleration techniques are used.
